What it does

  • 01 / 11

    Lazy chop

    Tap along. It chops where you tap.

  • 02 / 11

    Key detection

    It finds the sample's key, and how many cents it is off tune.

    Our own analyser (MIT): it matches the pitch-class profile (chroma) against the Albrecht–Shanahan key profiles, tuned on 158 key-labelled loops. A sound with no notes in it gets no key.

  • 03 / 11

    Repitch and keep tempo

    Repitch moves key and speed together, like the old samplers. Keep tempo moves the key only.

    Keep tempo is baked ahead with Rubber Band R3 (GPL); until the bake is ready you hear Signalsmith Stretch (MIT). Bounces use the same bake, so what you hear is what you get.

  • 04 / 11

    Crisp

    Hits stay sharp when keep tempo stretches. On for beats and drum loops, off for melodies, chords and voices.

    When on, each stretch between two hits is done on its own and every hit's attack keeps the original sound. Signalsmith Stretch (MIT).

  • 05 / 11

    Channel select

    Per sample: stereo, left, right, mid or side. Something you rarely find in other samplers.

    Mid = (L+R)/2, side = (L−R)/2. Picked where the sample comes in, before the engine, and never louder than the source. On the sampler screen.

  • 06 / 11

    Melody

    One slice across the keys, in the scale you pick.

  • 07 / 11

    Chop to MIDI

    Drag the chop order into your DAW.

  • 08 / 11

    Drum kit

    16 one-shots, each trimmed on its own.

  • 09 / 11

    fx rack

    Nine slots, from filter to space.

    Airwindows (MIT) models, plus our own punch, fuzz and filter.

  • 10 / 11

    Scratch

    Grab the record. Baby and chirp patterns too.

  • 11 / 11

    Day and night

    One button for the night look.

Change the engine, change the look

Two legendary samplers and an early-90s console's sound chip, modelled down to the circuits.

CLEAN · THE SAMPLE AS IT IS

Clear however far you stretch it.

BLACK · 12-BIT · 26 kHz

The legendary late-80s sampler. Gritty and heavy.

Built to the schematics: how it stores 12 bits, pitch by skipping and repeating samples, a different filter on each output, and the moving filter, which was checked against recordings of a real unit.

GREY · 12-BIT · 40 kHz

The other legendary late-80s sampler. Tight and warm.

Little is published about it, so these are estimates: its 12-bit storage, how it changes pitch, the output filter's shape and the noise level.

PURPLE · 4-BIT · 32 kHz

An early-90s console's sound chip. Round and hazy.

The chip's maths (BRR decoding, gaussian interpolation, 32,040 Hz) matches the open-source emulator ares bit for bit, across 7,300 test cases. The board's filter follows the schematic.

Shaped by hand, one decision at a time

flipper is not a copy of today's samplers. It was built by settling, one at a time, the things that got in the way of making beats.

01

Chop

Take one part of a sample and chop it until it sounds new. This is what flipper tries to do best.

  • 01 / 13

    Hits

    The hits are found once when the sample opens; move the sensitivity and it re-chops at once. A new sample comes in chopped at 50 %.

  • 02 / 13

    Beats

    Every 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2 or bar, at the sample's tempo.

  • 03 / 13

    Lazy chop

    It plays; tap a pad or any key and it cuts there. Slice 1 starts at the start. The whole take is one undo.

  • 04 / 13

    Early taps land

    It finds the hit from 80 ms before your tap to 60 ms after. On real drum loops 90–100 % of taps land on their hit.

  • 05 / 13

    Lit pad

    Only the next slice's pad lights up and cuts, for playing it the old machines' way.

  • 06 / 13

    The die

    A new chop on every roll. Locked markers and skipped parts stay; the rest is chopped anew.

  • 07 / 13

    Chaos

    Drag the die, 0–100 %. 0 % picks only clear hits, evenly; past 50 % chance creeps in; 100 % is pure chance. The pips show it.

  • 08 / 13

    By hand

    Double-click to add a marker (it snaps to a nearby hit), drag to move, drag across to pick several and delete. Alt: no snap.

  • 09 / 13

    Lock

    A locked marker stays where it is through every re-chop.

  • 10 / 13

    Skip

    A count-in or a breath is left out, not cut out. The slices after it move up; the file is untouched, so it can come back any time.

  • 11 / 13

    Start and end

    Push and pull each slice's start and end in ms; 0 is always 'as chopped'. With the magnet on, they land on a zero crossing within 10 ms.

  • 12 / 13

    Only five

    Up to 64 slices, each with pitch, reverse, level, fade in and fade out. Nothing more. Fades start at 0.

  • 13 / 13

    No clicks

    Every sound starts and ends on a 0.1 ms edge, so cutting mid-wave never ticks.

02

Key and tempo

Key and speed belong to the whole sample, not to each pad, so one sample can become a different track.

You get 89.9 BPM
Pitch it by −5 st +2 ct

  • 01 / 15

    Repitch

    Like tape, only the reading speed changes: pitch it down and it slows down and darkens. No stretching, so no digital artefacts.

  • 02 / 15

    How repitch reads

    clean reads through a sinc interpolator whose cutoff follows the speed: an octave up, what folds back sits at −85 dB. The old-machine engines read the machine's own rough way (skipping and repeating samples and so on), on purpose.

  • 03 / 15

    Keep tempo

    Length and pitch move apart: keep the tempo and move the key, or keep the key and move the tempo.

  • 04 / 15

    Two stretchers

    Rubber Band R3: 0 cents pitch error, pre-echo on hits at −26 to −37 dB, the cleanest but heavy. Signalsmith Stretch: within 5 cents, about 50× real time.

  • 05 / 15

    Picked by ear

    On key-only changes the two sounded close; on tempo-only changes R3 was clearly more natural. So what you hear is made with R3.

  • 06 / 15

    Baked ahead

    Change the key or speed and R3 bakes it in the background (a 4 s chop in about 0.5 s); the next hit plays the bake. Before that, and while a knob turns, Signalsmith plays at once.

  • 07 / 15

    One bake

    Not 64 bakes for 64 slices: the whole chopped range bakes once, so moving markers needs no new bake. Only slices with their own pitch bake apart.

  • 08 / 15

    Bake = bounce

    The bounce reads the same bake, so it sounds as heard. Bakes aren't saved; they're made again on load, and R3 bakes the same down to the bit every time.

  • 09 / 15

    Tape shortcut

    When speed and key move together like tape (within 0.1 cent) there is nothing to stretch, so it reads the repitch way: lighter, and exactly the old-sampler sound.

  • 10 / 15

    Sound on the hit

    The stretcher is primed so a pad sounds on the very sample it is hit. Many at once take turns, bridged by repitch while they wait.

  • 11 / 15

    Tempo maths

    Pitch it down and it shows the tempo you land on. Type the BPM you want and it gives the semitones and cents.

  • 12 / 15

    DAW tempo

    One key matches the song's tempo: the key in repitch, the speed in keep tempo.

  • 13 / 15

    Key chip

    The sample's key and the key it sounds in with the key knob, e.g. Am → Gm, and how many cents it is off tune.

  • 14 / 15

    Crisp

    Keep tempo stretches between the hits, each on its own, and keeps every hit's attack. On for beats, off for melodies.

  • 15 / 15

    Loop or one-shot

    Each sample is a loop (with a tempo) or a one-shot. The first guess comes from the file name and length; you can change it.

03

Play

The sampler moves like one deck. The pads don't each go their own way.

  • 01 / 11

    Pads

    16 or 32, with pages up to 64 slices. Pad 1 is C3.

  • 02 / 11

    Mono

    The default: a new pad cuts the last sound in 5 ms, like a chop played on one deck.

  • 03 / 11

    Poly

    Sounds overlap, for chords in melody mode.

  • 04 / 11

    While held

    It sounds as long as you hold it, and stops at the slice's own end.

  • 05 / 11

    Whole slice

    One hit plays it through to the next marker.

  • 06 / 11

    Melody

    One slice across the keys: by semitone, or major, minor, dorian, phrygian, mixolydian, harmonic minor, major and minor pentatonic.

  • 07 / 11

    White keys

    Pick it and every scale plays like C major on the white keys. 'Every key' is for pad controllers.

  • 08 / 11

    Root

    Not stuck on C3: move it with ‹ ›, or right-click a key to make it the root.

  • 09 / 11

    128 keys

    The screen keyboard holds every MIDI note. Scroll, zoom, and a click sends the real note.

  • 10 / 11

    Sustain

    The sustain pedal works in melody mode.

  • 11 / 11

    Panic

    The header key, or MIDI CC 120 and 123, fades every sound out in 5 ms.

04

Bring a sample in

Drop a file, or record the DAW's sound straight in as a sample.

  • 01 / 05

    Record

    Another track (side-chain) or flipper's own output (resample) becomes the new sample.

  • 02 / 05

    When, how long

    Now, or from the next bar's first beat. Until you stop, or 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars at the DAW's tempo.

  • 03 / 05

    Channel select

    Stereo, left, right, mid or side. Mid = (L+R)/2, side = (L−R)/2. Never louder than the source.

  • 04 / 05

    Whole sample gain

    One sample's level, after the engine and before the fx. Normalize sets it.

  • 05 / 05

    Any file name

    File names in any language open.

05

fx rack

Ten slots in the order the sound runs. All off at first; turn a knob up and it comes on. An off slot uses no CPU.

  • 01 / 13

    Filter

    Our own: LP, BP or HP, 12 or 24 dB, resonance. Clean, or a ladder that saturates a little. First in the rack or last.

  • 02 / 13

    Punch

    A one-knob compressor of our own, in feedback or classic.

  • 03 / 13

    Distort

    Five kinds: soft, tube, clip, fold, and our own fuzz. Runs 2× oversampled.

  • 04 / 13

    Crush

    Lowers the sample rate and cuts the bits, in steps or smooth.

  • 05 / 13

    Tape

    One knob of tape. Its flux is the small changes that keep tape alive.

  • 06 / 13

    Chorus

    An ensemble chorus: speed, range, mix.

  • 07 / 13

    Noise

    Dust, hiss and crackle, only while a sample sounds, so no noise is left in the gaps.

  • 08 / 13

    Echo

    Tape, digital (ping-pong too) or grain. Times in beats: 1/16 to a bar, dotted and triplet.

  • 09 / 13

    Space

    Five reverbs, from a small room to a huge space, with pre-delay and stereo width.

  • 10 / 13

    All

    The whole rack at once: input (a clipper), width, tone, output, fx on and off.

  • 11 / 13

    fx pads

    16 that act while held: red cut, yellow stutter, teal tape (stop, back, half, reverse), blue throw (LP, HP, echo, crush).

  • 12 / 13

    On the beat

    fx pads kick in now, on the next 1/16 or the next beat. Echoes and wobbles follow the DAW's beat, so a bounce sounds as heard.

  • 13 / 13

    Where it comes from

    Punch, filter, fuzz, bits, hiss, crackle and the digital and grain echoes are our own. The rest are Airwindows (MIT) models.

06

Turntable

Spin and scratch the chopped range like a record, in the sound of the engine you picked.

  • 01 / 10

    The record

    33⅓ rpm, one turn = 1.8 s. Repitch, keep tempo and the old-machine engines each read it their own way.

  • 02 / 10

    The hand

    Grab it with the mouse or a controller. The record moves only as far as your hand, so the needle never jumps, and moves are smoothed.

  • 03 / 10

    Slipmat

    Grabbed, it stops within a few ms; let go, it catches the platter within 25 ms.

  • 04 / 10

    Motor

    Start and stop times for ▶ ■: at once, or slowing down like a tape.

  • 05 / 10

    Fader

    A curve from smooth (for mixing) to sharp (for scratching), with the cut-in and a reversed setting.

  • 06 / 10

    Scratch patterns

    Baby, chirp, transformer, flare and more. Hold to scratch the slice on the record in that pattern.

  • 07 / 10

    16 keys

    16 keys pick slices 1–16 to scratch; harder hits push further. They start on the next 1/16.

  • 08 / 10

    Follow the song

    When the DAW plays, the record runs from its cue in time with the song, and finds its way back after you take over.

  • 09 / 10

    Controllers

    An endless knob is a jog, a strip is the hand, the pitch wheel pushes and pulls. MIDI learn works it out.

  • 10 / 10

    Automation

    The record, the hand and the fader are DAW parameters, so scratches end up in the bounce as played.

07

Drums

A screen for building a kit from 16 one-shots, inside the same plug-in as the sampler.

  • 01 / 08

    16 cells

    Drop a file in. Drop several and they fill in order from that cell.

  • 02 / 08

    An engine each

    clean, grey, black or purple, per cell.

  • 03 / 08

    Per cell

    Level, pan, pitch ±24, fade in and out. All automatable in the DAW.

  • 04 / 08

    Choke

    Groups 1–4: hit one and the last in its group stops, like open and closed hats.

  • 05 / 08

    16 outputs

    Send any cell to its own DAW track. Output 1 runs through the fx rack.

  • 06 / 08

    Echo and space

    Each cell sets only how much it sends; one heavy effect runs for all (CPU).

  • 07 / 08

    Key zones

    Cells from C6, or from C1 on MIDI channel 10, laid out never to clash with the sampler's pads.

  • 08 / 08

    Kit files

    A .flipkit holds the samples and settings. Opened in another project, only the kit comes in.

08

Out, and safe

What you make goes straight to the DAW, and nothing suddenly gets loud.

  • 01 / 08

    Chop to MIDI

    A clip that plays each slice at the moment the loop reaches it, dragged onto a DAW track. Start from the sample's own groove.

  • 02 / 08

    Bounce = live

    The same sound at block sizes from 1 to 4096, real time or offline.

  • 03 / 08

    MIDI learn

    Every control you perform with can be learned, as held or as a toggle.

  • 04 / 08

    .flip files

    The sample, the chop and the settings in one file. Older flips open sounding the same.

  • 05 / 08

    Undo

    One step per knob turn or drag, chops included.

  • 06 / 08

    The loudness rule

    A sudden loud sound is treated as the worst bug: the causes are fixed and tests guard them.

  • 07 / 08

    Safety limiter

    No limiter colours the everyday sound. The safety limiter at the very end (safe, vintage, off) is your choice.

  • 08 / 08

    Korean and English

    The whole screen in both. Day and night, and a skin for each engine.

Questions

Is it really free?

Yes. No money, no sign-up, and all of the source code is public.

Can I sell music I make with flipper?

Yes. The sounds and songs you make are yours. The licence covers the plug-in program, not what you make with it.

Will it run on my computer?

On Macs with Apple Silicon or Intel, and on 64-bit Windows 10 and 11. Not on ARM Windows yet.

Which DAWs?

Any DAW that opens AU or VST3 plug-ins. Checked in Logic Pro, REAPER and Cubase.

Anything to know about the beta?

macOS asks you to allow it once the first time; on Windows, click 'More info → Run anyway' in SmartScreen. When a new version is out, a dot lights up by the plug-in's logo.

Where is the source code?

On GitHub. The code we wrote is MIT; the plug-in we ship is AGPL-3.0 because of the libraries bundled with it.
